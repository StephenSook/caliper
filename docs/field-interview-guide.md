# Field interview guide

**Owner: Ryann.** Twenty minutes with a ResultsCX practitioner is worth more than
another feature. This is the highest value non coding task available and it is on
the critical path for two things we cannot get from the data.

---

## Before you start

**Ask permission to record, out loud, on the recording.** Something like: "Do you
mind if I record this so I get the details right? It stays with our team." If the
answer is no, take notes instead and do not record. A recorded conversation
without a recorded yes is unusable to us.

Then say what we are doing, in one sentence, because it makes the answers better:

> We are auditing the quality form itself as a measurement instrument, before we
> diagnose anybody from it. We already ran the statistics. We want to know what
> actually happens in the room.

**Never promise them anonymity you cannot deliver, and never use their name.** We
will refer to them as a senior ResultsCX practitioner, on every surface, forever.

---

## The seven questions, in priority order

Each one is designed so that either answer tells us something. Ask them in this
order and stop whenever you run out of time; the first three are the ones that
matter most.

### 1. When two evaluators score the same call and disagree, what happens?

**Why this is first.** We computed that **zero of seventeen calls were scored by
more than one evaluator**, and that the entire ability to compare the two
evaluators rests on two agents out of ten. We call that linkage fragility 2 of 10.

**What each answer buys us.** If the answer is "that does not really come up" or
"we do not usually double score", that is our central finding confirmed from the
inside, in their words, and it goes straight into the pitch. If the answer is
"we hold a calibration session", ask **how often, who attends, and whether the
result is written down anywhere**, because a calibration session that produces no
recorded statistic is still not a measured agreement.

### 2. Who wrote the questions on the form, and when were they last changed?

**Why.** We are arguing nobody has checked the instrument. If the form has not
been revised in years, that is the argument. If it was revised recently, ask
**what triggered it** and whether anything was measured before and after.

### 3. What actually happens to an agent who fails the same item repeatedly?

**Why.** This sets the stakes of the whole product, and we want it in their
language rather than ours. Listen for the escalation path: coaching, then a
documented plan, then something worse. **Do not lead them.** Ask it open and let
them describe the ladder.

### 4. How many calls per agent get evaluated in a typical month?

**Why.** We measured a mean of **1.7 evaluations per agent** in the supplied
sample, with five agents having exactly one. Industry cadence runs four to twelve
a month. If they say a number near 1.7, the sample is representative and our
refusal to diagnose individuals is about their real operation, not about a small
export. If they say eight, then the export is a slice and we must say so.

### 5. On the labor report, is the intended build baseline the 2.76 stage based
hours per curriculum hour, or the 17.47 team average with the buffer?

**Why.** This one is a straight blocker. The supplied labor report contains both,
they differ by more than six times, and they have different definitions. Our
USA versus Mexico versus Philippines model needs to name which one it uses. This
is worth asking even if you get nothing else.

### 6. When a training module gets built off a QA finding, does anyone go back
later and check whether that specific behaviour changed?

**Why.** Their own process document says there is no explicit post launch
evaluation step. We would like to hear whether that matches practice. If the
answer is "not really", it is the gap we built the product around, confirmed.

### 7. If you could change one thing about the form, what would it be?

**Why.** Ends on something they enjoy answering, and it occasionally produces the
single best line in a pitch. Let the silence sit after you ask it.

---

## What to do with the answers

1. **Hand Stephen the recording file.** It gets transcribed locally and never
   leaves the machine.
2. **Write down the three things that surprised you**, in your own words, within
   ten minutes of leaving. That memory decays fast.
3. Flag anything that **contradicts** what we computed. A contradiction is more
   valuable than a confirmation, because it is the thing that would have made us
   wrong on stage.

## What we will never do with them

- Name the person, their title, or their team on any judged surface.
- Quote them verbatim unless the wording is confirmed, because our transcription
  runs on a small local model and produces paraphrase.
- Put any agent name, member identifier, participation id or call id anywhere.

---

## Ryann's second job, if there is time

**Walk a stranger through the six screens and watch where they hesitate.**

Ten percent of the score is whether a non technical learning designer can read
every screen and act on it. The only way to know is to hand it to someone who has
not seen it and stay quiet. Note every place they pause, re read, or ask what a
word means. Each of those is a caption we have not written yet.

Do not explain the screen while they are reading it. The moment you explain, the
test is over.
